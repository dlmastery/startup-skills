## "Audited improvement" becomes the unit the field reports in: top ML venues and $1T+ in systematic capital now require deflated-metrics disclosure, with Ascent attestation the de facto format

SAN FRANCISCO — September 2032 — Six years after a solo founder shipped a rigor layer for an orphaned open-source research loop, the way empirical results are reported has changed shape. Three of the four major ML conferences now require a trial-count disclosure — the raw headline number alongside its deflated counterpart and the full experiment ledger, discards included — for any paper claiming a metric improvement. The largest allocators ask systematic funds the same question examiners ask banks: *show me the ledger behind the backtest.* In both cases, the artifact most commonly attached is an Ascent attestation: a fingerprinted winner archive whose gate results, embargo geometry, and trial count a stranger can verify by rerunning it with one command.

The convention has a name practitioners use without attribution now: reporting in **audited improvements** — not "we improved AUROC by two points" but "two kept, audited improvements, ledger attached." The phrase came from the constitution's original unit of value: one audited, kept improvement to the user's metric.

"The thing I never say out loud in committee anymore is 'trust me,'" said the head of research at a systematic fund who runs forty researchers on Ascent Team. "A pitched strategy arrives as a replayable campaign — every variant, every discard, the trial count the deflation was computed from. The meeting that used to take three hours takes twenty minutes, and the strategy that can't produce its ledger doesn't get the meeting."

"I was the person the automation literature warned about — I preprocessed on the full dataset before splitting, and I didn't know it," said a computational biologist whose 2027 paper carried one of the first Ascent validation records in a life-science venue. "I didn't adopt it because I trusted AI. I adopted it because it was the first system that assumed I *shouldn't* trust anyone's analysis, including my own."

"The honest number turned out to be the sales pitch," said one early user, a solo quant who pre-ordered the CLI for $99 in 2026. "They published their flagship Sharpe *with the deflation attached* — '+6.52 raw, here's what survives the trial-count correction, here's the ledger.' Every vendor before them led with the flattering number. I gave my card to the first one that led with the correction."

Ascent's own disclosure practice — publishing deflated numbers before critics could compute them — is now cited in journal reviewer guidelines as the template for post-hoc-selection disclosure. The company's SOTA catalog lists reproducible champion bundles across several hundred public datasets, each with a reproduction count; the reproduction network, not advertising, remains the primary way new users arrive.
